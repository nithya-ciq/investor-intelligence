③ Anticipated VC questions — rehearse answers
9 questions that will come up at least once across the 5 conversations
"Why BevAlc specifically — why not horizontal CRM?"
→ Three-tier distribution is mandated by law in most US states. The workflow constraints are different from generic CRM — supplier-to-distributor commitments, depletion tracking, on-premise vs off-premise routing. Horizontal CRM (Salesforce) can't model this without massive customization. Vertical specialization is the moat.
"Why now? What changed?"
→ Two simultaneous shifts: (1) Multi-agent orchestration matured (CrewAI/AutoGen/LangGraph in 2024-25). (2) BevAlc category contraction has supplier commercial teams under real cost pressure — finally willing to consider non-Salesforce tooling.
"What's the team — why you specifically?"
→ Shyam (CEO) — operator background. Srini (CPO) — multi-agent architecture, built Spike + Hype as parallel proof-of-concept dogfood. Gauri — BevAlc industry network. Hiral — HR/talent. Compact senior team, India cost base, capital-efficient by design.
"Who are your design partners / first customers?"
→ Have 2-3 names ready. Sera Luce is the validated reference. Mention any active pilot conversations. If pre-customer: name the supplier segments you're talking to (e.g. premium spirits importers, regional craft producers).
"How big is the wedge — what's the ARR ceiling on year 1 customers?"
→ Have a number ready. ~3,000 BevAlc suppliers in US importing or producing premium product · avg commercial team size 5-30 reps · target $X/seat/month → $Y per customer ARR. Multiply by realistic year-1 customer count.
"Why won't Salesforce/Crunchtime/Lilypad/Encompass crush you?"
→ They're vertical SaaS, not vertical agentic AI. Their architecture is forms-and-workflows; ours is agents-that-make-decisions. The vertical depth they have means migration friction, not a structural moat — agentic AI rebuilds the workflow itself, not just the data model.
"How much are you raising? Lead vs syndicate?"
→ Have your actual raise number ready before walking in. Whatever the target is — say it crisply, with months-of-runway tied to it, and your lead-vs-syndicate posture. Have a number, not a range that drifts. Different VCs will probe this differently: Drew (AV) cares because they don't lead — needs to know who the lead is. Tyler (Presidio) cares because pre-seed is below their stage. Rachel (Red Bike) cares because they write specific seed-size checks.
"What's the most concerning thing about your business right now?"
→ Pick ONE real concern. Honesty signals operator maturity. Suggested: "Sales cycle length — BevAlc suppliers have long compliance review cycles, so customer-acquisition timing is real. Our mitigation is starting with the smallest decision (single-agent pilot) to compress the first 'yes' into 30 days."
"What does the next 6 months look like if you take our money?"
→ Concrete milestones: 3 paying customers by Q3, $X ARR run-rate by Q4, hiring (1 ML eng, 1 sales). End with the Series A trigger: "$1M ARR + 5 customers = ready for Series A conversations."
④ Reverse-pitch listening playbook — what to capture in their 5 minutes
During each VC's 5-min pitch, take notes on these 4 things
  1. Specific check size they call out — many VCs name ranges publicly that aren't on their website. Capture verbatim. Use it in your 1:1: "you said $X-$Y — we're a Z fit at $X."
  2. Specific portfolio company they cite — they'll mention 1-2 favorites. Reference one in your opening if relevant: "your story about [Daash Intelligence / HeyMilo / Materia] is exactly the pattern BevGenie sits in."
  3. What they say they're "actively looking for" — this is the real signal. They might say "agentic infrastructure plays" or "vertical AI in regulated industries" or "founders with operator backgrounds." Echo their phrase back to them in the 1:1.
  4. One sentence that's NOT on their website — VCs at these events test new framings. If they say something that sounds fresh (e.g. "we're really focused on agents that have authority to commit dollars"), they're proud of it. Quoting it back signals you were listening.
⑤ Physical + logistics
Bring + wear + carry
  • 10–15 printed one-pagers — paper still wins for handoff. Crisp PDF print, color, no creases. Keep in a slim folder, not loose in a backpack.
  • 20+ business cards — name + title + email + linkedin + a tiny tagline ("Agentic intelligence for BevAlc supplier teams"). NYC events run through cards fast.

  • Phone with full battery + portable charger — for capturing names, LinkedIn-adding on the spot, and finding the venue.
  • Dress code: business casual — NYC startup events lean clean-but-not-suit. Button-down or quality knit, dark pants, leather shoes. Avoid logo'd clothing (no Twenty20/ConsumerIQ shirts — wear them post-funding).

  • Don't eat heavy before — light meal 2 hours prior. Reverse-pitch events run 3-4 hours; energy management matters.
⑥ Networking strategy — beyond the 5 on stage
The other founders in the room are 50% of the value
  • Founders pitching alongside you are warm-intro sources to other VCs — many will have spoken to investors you'd want. Trade notes. "Who else have you been talking to that's been a great conversation?" is a high-yield question.
  • Look for B2B AI / vertical-SaaS founders specifically — they're peers; their advisor lists overlap with what you'd want.
  • Sumitomo / Plum Alley / Alumni Ventures employees not pitching — they'll be in the room. Junior team members often have better signal on which deals their fund is actually engaging on.
  • Don't pitch to other founders — they're not buyers and not capital. Trade tactics, sources, and intros. Build the peer network you'll lean on at Series A.
  • Capture every name in real-time — note 3 details after each conversation (name, role, what they care about). Memory will be useless 4 hours in.
⑦ Red flags + traps to avoid
Watch for these in real-time
  • VC's stated thesis ≠ what they actually invest in — sometimes the stage they pitch isn't the stage they write checks at. If Drew Collins says AV writes $5-10M checks but their actual seed check is $250K from the syndicate, that matters. Probe gently in the 1:1.
  • "Send me your deck and I'll get back to you" — soft no. Counter with: "Happy to send — what's the criterion you'd use to decide if it's interesting?" Forces them to commit or politely decline now.
  • "This is interesting but not for our current fund" — translation: "no." Don't fight; pivot to: "Who would you point me to?" Treat the conversation as introduction-fishing.
  • Strategic-investor pressure (especially Presidio) — corporate VCs sometimes ask for ROFRs, info rights, or commercial commitments. Don't agree to anything in the room. "Let me come back to you with a full term sheet review."
  • Talking too much — 60-sec opener, then ASK A QUESTION about their thesis. The founder who listens 60% and talks 40% wins. Most founders invert this.
⑧ The 18-hour rule — follow-ups
Every follow-up email goes out within 18 hours of the conversation
  • Personalize the first sentence — quote something specific they said in their reverse pitch OR in your 1:1. Generic "great to meet you" emails get archived.
  • Attach the 5-min deck — not the one-pager. The deck shows depth.
  • Propose ONE concrete next step — not "let me know if useful." Specific options: 30-min deep-dive call, intro to a portfolio peer, attend their portfolio CEO dinner, etc.
  • Track everything in a CRM — dogfood BevGenie if it can handle this, otherwise a clean spreadsheet. Date / contact / status / next step / followup date.
